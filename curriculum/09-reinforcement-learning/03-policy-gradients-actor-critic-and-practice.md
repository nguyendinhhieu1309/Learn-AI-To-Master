# Policy Gradients, Actor-Critic, and Practical RL

Value-based methods learn how good actions are. Policy-gradient methods learn a policy directly. They are central to continuous control and many modern reinforcement-learning systems.

## 1. The policy objective

A stochastic policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ assigns action probabilities. We want to maximize expected discounted return:

$$
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^t r_t\right]
$$

The policy-gradient theorem gives an estimator proportional to:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,A^\pi(s_t,a_t)\right]
$$

The advantage $A^\pi$ measures whether an action was better than the policy’s usual action in that state. Increasing the log-probability of positive-advantage actions and reducing it for negative-advantage actions improves expected return in expectation.

## 2. REINFORCE and variance reduction

REINFORCE uses sampled returns as a noisy advantage estimate. It is simple but high variance. A baseline $b(s)$ independent of the sampled action can be subtracted without changing the expected gradient:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,(G_t-b(s_t))\right]
$$

A learned value function $V_\phi(s)$ is a common baseline. Normalize advantages within a batch only as a practical stabilization choice; it changes the scale and sometimes behavior of the update.

## 3. Actor-critic

The actor is the policy; the critic estimates value or action value. A one-step temporal-difference residual is:

$$
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)
$$

It can estimate the advantage. The critic is trained to predict returns or TD targets, while the actor is updated using the critic’s estimate. If the critic is inaccurate, biased policy updates can result; monitor critic loss and value predictions rather than assuming the actor loss tells the full story.

Generalized Advantage Estimation combines TD residuals with a parameter $\lambda$ to trade bias for variance:

$$
\hat{A}_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}
$$

## 4. On-policy and off-policy learning

On-policy methods such as PPO learn from data generated by the current or recent policy. Off-policy methods can reuse replayed experience, improving sample efficiency, but need to handle distribution mismatch and stale data. Importance sampling can correct action-probability mismatch but may have high variance.

PPO clips the ratio between new and old policy probabilities to discourage excessively large updates. The clipped objective is a practical heuristic; it does not guarantee a globally safe update or solve reward misspecification.

## 5. Continuous actions

For continuous control, a policy may output the parameters of a Gaussian distribution, often a mean and log standard deviation. Actions may be squashed with `tanh` to fit bounds; account for the transformation when computing log probabilities. Deterministic actor-critic methods such as TD3 and SAC are designed for continuous action spaces; SAC also optimizes an entropy term to encourage exploration.

## 6. Reward design and exploration

Reward shaping can speed learning but can also create shortcuts that differ from the task. Use task-level evaluation, inspect behavior, and prefer potential-based shaping when its assumptions apply. Track reward components and termination reasons separately.

Exploration may use action noise, entropy regularization, randomized starts, or environment variation. A policy can exploit simulator bugs or survive by never attempting the intended behavior. Test in varied conditions and compare against simple policies.

## 7. Practical training protocol

1. Verify observations, action bounds, reward scale, reset behavior, and termination/truncation flags.
2. Run random and simple scripted baselines.
3. Fix seeds and evaluate several independent training runs.
4. Separate training episodes from evaluation episodes; disable exploration during evaluation when appropriate.
5. Plot return distribution, success rate, episode length, constraint violations, and task-specific metrics.
6. Test generalization across initial states and environment parameters.
7. For real robots, use simulation, safety constraints, conservative deployment, and emergency stops.

One successful seed is not reliable evidence. RL results vary substantially with random seed, environment version, and evaluation procedure.
